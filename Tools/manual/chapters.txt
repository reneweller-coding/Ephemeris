== About | | - ==

Ephemeris composes and plays long pieces in the manner of the modern Berlin School: sequencer lines of
different lengths turning against each other over a common root, a bass that holds the ground, tape
choirs and string machines, a lead that sings over the peak, the room of a tape echo and a long hall.
Nothing is played back from a recording: every sound is synthesised by the engine -- the only recorded material
are the single cycles some of the pad synth's wavetables are made of -- and every note is decided by the composer.

The same seed always gives the same piece. A piece is a seed, a style, a length and a handful of
rerolls, which is what a set file stores and what you can hand to someone else. The standalone and the
plugin play what the offline renderer plays, sample for sample.

An ephemeris is the table that gives the positions of the heavenly bodies for every date. A sequencer
is such a table, and the music here is made of orbits of different lengths around one root that meet
again only now and then. The Rack page draws them that way.

== Signal flow | | - ==

[image flow.png: The signal flow: every unit a box in the colour of its family, the groups as frames, the buses as arrows.]

What plays the instruments comes in at the top: the composer, who writes the form, the harmony, the sounds and the mix
of every piece; the rack, eight sequencer rows and the transposer that moves them; the moves, the automation of a
player's two hands on the knobs; and you, on the Perform page or with MIDI. The ten modular voices -- the rows, the lead and the drone --
run side by side in the lanes of the processor's vector registers at twice the sample rate: two oscillators after one
of six classic designs, a driven mixer, one of ten filters solved as circuits, envelopes, LFOs and a matrix. The other
sources play beside them. Every source has a channel strip that places it in depth and in the stereo field and sends
it into the rooms; the master bus keeps the lows mono, the width in check and the peaks under -1 dBTP, and a
correction measured on the piece's loudest part brings every piece to its style's level.

== The top bar | | - ==

Style, key and scale choose the piece's character: Cosmic, Doom, Melodic, Modern and Drift are five
style profiles with their own tempi, forms, layers and spaces. Beside them, three buttons choose what is
composed: Piece -- a single piece; Concert -- pieces strung together into a concert that travels through
related keys; Night set -- pieces in mixed styles, each mixed into the next as a DJ does it. Length is the
length of what is chosen: a piece's 4 to 40 minutes, a concert's or a night set's 20 minutes to 12 hours. A
button composes what it names at once if what plays is something else; Compose names what it makes (Compose
piece, Compose concert, Compose night set) and is lit while what plays is not what the buttons choose. The
status row says what plays: a single piece, or a concert or a night set of so many pieces. With
Morph To (on the Master page) a concert also wanders from its style to another: each piece is composed
between the two, the further into the concert the nearer the second. Concert Arc gives a concert an arc
of tension: its first and last pieces quieter -- fewer rows at the peak, the lead and the drums rarer, the
moves slower, the sound a little darker -- and the pieces around 60 % of the way the densest.
Compose writes it with the current seed, the die draws another one, Play (the triangle, a square while it plays)
starts and stops the standalone (in a host the host's transport plays), the speaker silences it. Undo, Redo, Help and the settings are at the
right of the row below (The panel).

A night set (up to twelve hours) is played as a DJ plays one. The pieces last
eight to fifteen minutes and their styles climb and descend a ladder -- Drift, Doom, Cosmic, Modern,
Melodic -- one rung at a time, along waves of energy of 80 to 120 minutes over the night's own arc (the
densest around 60 % of the way), never three pieces of one style in a row; the style in the top bar is the
first. No piece ends in silence: each one's last 32 to 40 bars are an outro in which its rows play on, and
the next piece starts over it, on the other half of the rack (rows 1-4 and 5-8 take turns). First its
intro, 8 to 16 bars -- the drone slides into the new key, a fourth or a fifth away (now and then a minor
third), with its pads and its air -- then its bass, alone for eight bars: there the old piece's counter
rows stop and its bass gives up its lows on the EQ and recedes, as a DJ swaps the bass. Then the new main
sequence comes in and fills up while the old one recedes to its end. The mix is beat-matched: the new
piece takes the tempo the old one ended in, within 6 BPM of its style's range, and ramps to its own only
after the handover (16 bars for every 4 BPM). The level stays the old piece's until its end, and Doom and
Drift are brought 1.2 dB nearer to the louder styles. A piece of a night set has at most four rows.
Composing eight hours takes about half a second; export writes the whole night.

Scale offers eight modes: Aeolian, Dorian, Phrygian, Harmonic Minor, Minor Pentatonic, Mixolydian, Lydian
and Locrian. The harmony is the Berlin School's: a centre held by the drone and the bass, chords that
change rarely. From the first build on, a chord track moves the bass row alone under the unchanged
sequence -- the counter rows are heard anew over the wandering bass -- in one of four ways the style
draws: one chord for the whole phase, a pendulum between two (i-VII, i-VI, i-iv, i-IV in Dorian, i-bII in
Phrygian), a loop of three or four (i-VI-VII, i-VII-VI, i-iv-VI ...) or a walk by a table of chances.
Drift and Modern stay longest on one chord, Melodic changes every four to eight bars. The tape keys and
the strings build their chords on the same degree: the choirs mostly plain triads, the strings open
fifths, triads, sus chords, add9, stacked fourths and m9 as well. The transposer moves the whole sequence
a fifth up, a fourth up or a minor third down and back, only on the plateau and at the peak, while the
drone stays on the centre. At some peaks Aeolian brightens to Dorian, in some breakdowns it darkens to
Phrygian, and every piece ends on the open fifth. A later phase may move its key by a fourth, a tone or a
third, and take a minor mode of its own, as the parts of a suite do.

The reroll buttons (on the Arrange page) draw one part of the piece again and leave everything else as it was, to
the note: the form, the tempo, the rows' shapes, the rack's patterns, the layers, the lead, the chords and pads, the
moves on the knobs, and the sounds. The disk and the folder on the Export page save and load a piece as a small text file;
Export writes the piece offline as a 24-bit WAV and a MIDI file beside it, with the moves as controllers -- and, if
you choose so, the stems: a WAV per channel strip and one for the rooms in a folder beside the mix, whose sum is the
mix before the master.

The panel. Every synth's page is laid out as an instrument's panel: its controls in titled groups --
Oscillators, Wavetable, Filter, the Amp, Filter and Mod Envelopes, the LFOs and the Mod Matrix, Mix, Sends, and on
the string machine Registers, Tone, Envelope and Ensemble as on a Streichfett -- and the controls a hand goes to first as large encoders: the cutoff and the
resonance, the registration and the ensemble, the pad synth's place in its table, the level. The colours say what a
control does, the same on every page -- they are the logo's five planets: amber for the sources (oscillators,
tapes, registers), copper for the filters, sage for the envelopes, teal for everything that moves by itself (LFOs,
scans, ensembles, the echoes' time), steel blue for the room and the mix; the sun's amber marks the page in front
and the transport. A page taller than the window scrolls. The standalone's window can be maximised, and full
screen (F11, or in the settings; Esc leaves it) fills the screen; the whole panel grows with the window. The update
check (in the settings) asks GitHub once a day whether a newer Ephemeris is out and shows it in the status row as a
link to its page -- nothing else is sent, nothing is downloaded; switched off, it asks nothing. The synths that share
a tab -- the row voices, the lead, the drone, the poly synth, the tape keys and the strings under Synths, the echoes
and the hall under Rooms, the console and the master under Mixer -- each have a small tab of their own. A jump on the arrange view finds the notes that sound on across it -- the
drone, the pads, a long lead note -- and starts them where it lands.

Below, the arrange view shows the sections of the piece -- the atmosphere at the start, the entry of the
rows, the builds, the lead, the peak, the bridges, the fade -- with the playhead, a lane per layer lit where it
has notes, and a ruler of bars (of minutes for a concert or a night set). Click to jump. A concert's pieces are
apart by a line and carry their number (a night set's its style as well).

[image screenshots/concert.png: A concert of four pieces in the arrange view: their numbers at their starts, the ruler in minutes.]

The mouse wheel zooms in and out around the pointer, down to four bars; zoomed in, the lanes show the notes
themselves, a bar along the bottom shows where the window lies in the whole, and the window pages on with the
playhead -- unless it was moved away from it. A drag, Shift with the wheel or the wheel sideways moves along; a
double click shows the whole length again.

[image screenshots/zoom.png: The arrange view zoomed in on forty bars of a build: the rows' notes, the pads, the drone, the bar numbers.]

== The panel | | - ==

Ephemeris shares its panel with the other generators of the family -- Phosphene, Totality and Parhelion (and, in part, Noctuary) -- so that what is learned on one holds
on the others: the same places, the same keys, the same controllers, the same hands. Each keeps its own colours, its
own letters and the picture behind its panel (Settings > Picture behind the panel takes it away).

The header's first row: the logo, the style, the key and the scale, what is composed and its length, Compose, the
die (a new seed), Play and the speaker (mute). Its second row: the status line, and at the right Undo, Redo, Help and the settings.
Under the header the whole piece, concert or night set at a glance; a click jumps there. The tabs come in one order everywhere: Set,
Arrange, the instrument's own pages -- a page of several has small tabs of its own --, Mixer, Perform, Export, Style.

The unambiguous actions are icons since 03.10.2026, their words in the tooltips: the die draws a new seed, the triangle plays and the square stops, the speaker mutes (crossed out while muted), the disk saves and the folder loads, the MIDI socket learns a controller (Perform).

A page taller than the window shows its groups in sections, one at a time, with a switch at its top right: a synth's
sound and its modulation apart (Sound, Modulation), and where even those do not fit, cut further, each named after its
first group (Filter, LFO, Matrix ...). At the window's usual size, 1280 by 860, no page scrolls. The overview above the
tabs can be folded away in the settings; the pages then get its height.

A colour says what a control does, the same on every page and in every generator of the family: the sources, the
filters, the envelopes, what moves by itself, the room and the mix. A knob the composer moves has a thin ring round
it that shows where it stands at this moment. A double click sets a control to its default; a right click offers
MIDI learn, forgetting the controller, and the default.

Undo and Redo take back and bring back what was changed -- a knob, a new seed, a reroll, a preset, a loaded set --
one step at a time; the button's tooltip names the step. Help opens this manual by topic, with the page in front, the
keys and the headset as topics of their own. The settings hold the update check (it asks GitHub once a day whether a
newer Ephemeris is out; nothing else is sent), the picture behind the panel, the overview, the headset, the window's
size, full screen, the keys and About.

    Space                 play / stop
    Ctrl+Z                undo
    Ctrl+Y, Ctrl+Shift+Z  redo
    F1                    help
    F11                   full screen (the standalone); Esc leaves it and closes the help
    Ctrl+S, Ctrl+O        save the set, open one
    Ctrl+E                export

The controllers every generator of the family listens to without learning: controller 74 (brightness) moves the rows' filters, 11 (the expression pedal) throws the echo, 64 (the sustain pedal) holds the moves; the mod wheel is left free.

The headset. The controls of a Meta Quest's hands are shown only while a headset sends them -- or always, if the
settings say so (Headset > Always show them); Off stops listening. The Quest app sends its hands to this computer in
bridge mode: bridge_host = this computer's address in its eph.cfg (audio=0 leaves the headset silent), and Ephemeris
listens on port 9102. The hands mean the same in every generator of the family, on the headset alone and here:

    left pinch            play / stop
    both hands pinched    the next piece (a new seed)
    right pinch           hold the moves, or let them go again (Hold Moves)
    left hand up, down    the rows' filters, two octaves either way
    right hand up         the echo throw

A pinch acts when it opens again, so a pinch of both hands is never also one of each; a hand moves its control only
while it does not pinch, and both glide and are centred: a hand at mid height plays what was composed.

== Set | compose | 0 ==

The composer's settings: the style, the key, the scale, the tempo and whether the style chooses it, the lengths of a
piece, a concert and a night set, Morph To, Concert Arc and Album Form -- the top bar shows the first of them as well.
Their meaning is told under The top bar and Master.

== Mixer | | 13 ==

A channel strip per source, in the order the engine mixes them: the eight rows, the lead, the drone,
the tape keys, the string machine, the drums and the atmosphere. Each strip has its pan (where the
source has one), its sends to the tape echo and to the hall -- the rows also to the second echo -- its
fader and its meter. The controls are the same parameters the sources' own pages show. Show sends unfolds
the sends of every strip (the echoes, the early reflections, the blend room, the hall, the shimmer); folded,
the console shows level, pan and meter only, with long faders.

The composer sets the stereo field for each piece: the bass in the middle, the main sequence near it, the
other counter rows 30 to 50 % out to alternating sides, the lead a little off the middle. It keeps the bass
dry and the tape keys and strings out of the echo, and sends the counter rows after the first into the
second echo, so each layer of the sequence has a delay time of its own. It sets all this on the faders, pans and
sends themselves as the piece begins -- the mixer shows the piece's mix, and a hand that moves a fader moves it from
there; its moves along the form (the bass coming closer, the pads stepping back while the lead plays) run from those
settings. As with the sounds, a new piece that keeps a strip's settings keeps your changes to it.

Every source has a Low Cut on its own page: a gentle high pass (12 dB per octave) that keeps it out of the
bands below its own, after the production guide's frequency plan. Only the bass row reaches down to 30 Hz;
the composer starts the main sequence at 90 Hz and the other counter rows at 200 Hz; the lead and the tape
keys start at 150 Hz, the strings at 200 Hz, the atmosphere at 120 Hz, the drone at 40 Hz (its lowest note
is at 82 Hz), the drums at 25 Hz. Mud between 200 and 500 Hz and a crowded bass are what make a long
piece tiring; this is where they are kept away.

Punch (on the rows) lifts a row's attacks by up to 6 dB and leaves its sustain: the composer gives it to the
main sequence, which keeps it in front. Distance (on the rows, the lead, the drone, the tape keys and the strings) places a source between near (0)
and the horizon (1) with every cue at once, so nothing is near in its level and far in the hall: at 0.3 it
is 4 dB quieter, its highs end at 10 kHz and it sends a little more to the hall; at 0.6 it is 10 dB back,
dark from 5 kHz and well in the hall; at 1 it is 24 dB back, 2.5 kHz and almost only hall. The composer
moves the bass with it: at each entry it comes in from 0.7 over 45 seconds, in the breakdown it recedes over
up to two minutes, and in the coda before it stops -- an approach and a leaving instead of a fader ride.

The rows also make room for themselves: while they play, the pads (strings and tape keys) step back in their
middle, by up to Cascade Duck (Master page, 2.5 dB) -- in six bands between 350 Hz and 4.8 kHz, each only
where the rows have energy in it -- and the pads in turn push the atmosphere back the same way: the pads stay
full while the sequence rests. The rows reach the mix through a resonance suppressor (Resonance Tamer, Master
page): in the same six bands, a band that stands more than 6 dB over the others is pulled back, up to 3:1, so a
filter's resonance sweeping through never sticks out or tires the ear.

The meter reads what the source puts into the mix after its fader and pan: a green bar for the RMS
level with a release of 300 ms, turning amber above -6 dBFS and red above 0, and a peak line that holds
for a second and a half before it falls at 20 dB per second. The figure under the meter is the held
peak. A strip that shows -inf is not playing at the moment.

== Perform | perform | 15 ==

The performer's controls act on the piece as it plays, and at their defaults they do nothing at all:
the piece is then exactly as composed. Filter moves the rows' filters up to two octaves either way, as
a hand on the cutoff knobs of a modular would. Transpose is the transposition key: every note that
starts after a change comes in transposed; notes that sound keep their pitch. Hold Moves lets go of the
knobs: the moves the composer wrote stand where they are until you release it. Echo Throw sends
every source into the tape echo and raises the echo's feedback with it, for a dub throw at the end of
a phrase.

A MIDI keyboard plays the transposition key directly: a key transposes by its distance from middle C
and holds until the next key, as the transposer of a sequencer did. Controller 74 (brightness) grabs the
filters, the expression pedal (11) throws the echo, the sustain pedal (64) holds the moves -- the same controllers
in every generator of the family. The MIDI socket beside each binds any controller to it; the bindings are saved with the
plugin's state. While a headset sends its hands, a box beside the controls shows what they do (The panel).

The keyboard (the Keyboard group): Keyboard Plays sends the keys of a MIDI keyboard to a voice instead of the
transposition -- the lead, the drone, the poly synth, the tape keys, the strings, the drums (General MIDI's drum map), a
row, or by channel (1 to 8 the rows, 9 the lead, 10 the drums, 11 the drone, 12 the poly synth, 13 the tape keys, 14 the
strings) -- with the sound its page has, untransposed. Replace leaves that voice's
generated notes out, Layer plays over them; Composer off leaves every generated note out, and only what is played
sounds, through the mix and the effects as composed. The keys play while the music runs, each on its sample; an export
plays what was composed.

Three options of the Keyboard group, each off until you choose it: Lower Keys Play splits the keyboard -- the keys
below Split At (C1 to C5, C3 to begin with) play the voice chosen there, the keys from it upwards the voice of Keyboard
Plays; Scale Lock moves a key outside the piece's scale to the nearest note inside it, so no wrong note can sound; Velocity
Curve shapes the touch -- As Played, Soft (a light touch is enough), Hard (it takes a firm one) or Fixed (every note
at 100, for pads that send no velocity). A key's release always ends the note its press started, even when an option
changed while it was held.

A row cannot be mutated live: the piece is composed before it plays. To hear the rack with other
patterns, reroll the rack.

== Rack | row | 2 ==

On the left, the orrery. Every row that plays notes is a planet on an orbit, the bass innermost; one
turn is one cycle of the row, its length in steps times its step. The top of every orbit is the row's
first step. When running rows stand there together, a line of light rises from the centre: a
conjunction, the moment the patterns come round together. Under the orbits, each row's cycle and the
time until all running rows meet again. The sun in the middle is the transposer: its letter is the root
the rows play on at the moment, and it moves when the transposer does.

The composer uses the conjunctions: a build, which brings in a new counter row, ends where that row and
the bass row begin their cycles together again, if such a bar is near.

On the right, the rows' parameters: length and step, direction, octave and transposition, the chance
of a mutation (a Turing machine's shift register) -- weighed once every sixteen bars, so that a pattern
holds at least that long, as a hypnotic one must -- the gate, level, pan and sends, the filter sweep, and
the mode -- notes, or the transposer that moves the others. The composer sets the rows for each piece;
the values here are what a piece starts from.

The composer draws each row from one of the Berlin School's figures: the octave pendulum, the fifth
anchor, the 3+1 pulse (three notes and a rest), stairs up and down, the mode's colour (the note that
makes Dorian or Phrygian heard), the Phrygian push, the arpeggio spiral from i to VI, or a canon of the
row before -- three steps later or a fifth up -- besides the older free rules. The bass ostinato has eight
or sixteen steps and a long gate; every eight steps have one to three rests (the bass none to two), the
first always sounds on the root or the fifth, accents fall on the first and fifth step or 3+3+2. The
bass and the main sequence hold the ground: they change at half the style's rate and never by chance. On
the other counter rows some steps are probability gates that sound on some rounds only -- Modern has the
most, Cosmic and Doom the fewest; a few steps, the most in Drift and Modern, play a new note each time
round, drawn through a quantiser from the root, fifth, octave, seventh, fourth and third of the mode. A
counter row comes in with six of its steps silent and fills a step every two bars. On the plateau and at
the peak a few steps are ratcheted into quick triggers, at some peaks the eighths row doubles its pulse,
and in the breakdown and the coda the bass loses its steps one by one.

What makes a sequence hypnotic is less its notes than its timbre moving against them. Every row carries
two modulation sequencers beside its notes: a lane of 3, 5, 7 or 12 steps -- never the row's own length --
that opens the filter on a few notes and closes it on others, and a second one of 3 to 12 steps that
lengthens the filter's decay on some notes and plucks others short. The accents of timbre wander through
the pattern and meet it again only after many rounds (Modern the deepest, Drift the shallowest). Over that,
Filter Sweep turns the row's cutoff slowly up and down, a sine of its own period between 39 and 79 seconds
for every row, never in step with the bars or with each other: the long openings and closings of the
Berlin School. The composer gives the main sequence the widest sweep and a squelchy voice -- more
resonance, a short deep filter envelope, strong accents -- the bass a round one and the other rows
something between; Motion on the Master page scales every sweep with the other slow movements. And on every
counter row one knob of its timbre wanders through the piece in glides of one to three minutes -- the place in
its wavetable, else the mix of saw and pulse, or the pulse width where the pulse is heard -- as does the pad
synth's place in its table: a spectrum that never stands still for long. The bass keeps its sound, the ground.

No sequence runs more than sixteen bars unchanged. The composer plans the variations the way a player at
the sequencer makes them, one at a time, every eight to sixteen bars: a step switched off or on, a step
thrown an octave up, the row shortened by a step or two for four or eight bars (the sequencer skipping),
its direction turned backwards or into a pendulum for as long, or the row taken back to its theme once
two things have changed -- mostly on the main sequence, now and then on another counter row or the bass.
Before some peaks there is a break: every row and the drums stop for two or four bars, the echoes' tails,
the pads and the lead go on with the echo's feedback raised, and everything comes back together on the
peak's downbeat (Melodic and Modern in three pieces of five, Drift in one). One to three times a piece
the tape echo switches from its dotted eighths to eighths or quarter triplets for a section.

== Arrange and automation | | 1 ==

The Arrange page: the rerolls, which draw one part of the piece again (The top bar), and the automation -- the moves,
what the player's two hands do to the knobs over the whole piece. Every knob the composer moves has a lane:
its offset from where you set the knob, as a curve over the piece, from -1 to +1 of the knob's range --
the filter of the bass row opening towards the peak and closing in the coda, the counter rows' filters,
the lead's, the echo's feedback thrown now and then, the wind rising and falling in the atmosphere. The
colour is the hand that moves the knob.

Above the lanes, a bar per hand shows where it is busy. A player has two hands, and the composer keeps to
that: never more than two knobs move at once, and a hand finishes one move before it starts the next. The
sections of the piece lie behind the lanes, the playhead runs over them, and a click jumps there. The moves
can be held on the Perform page (Hold Moves): every knob then stays where the curve has it.

== Voices | voice | 3 ==

Each row plays its own modular voice: two drifting oscillators (a sawtooth blended into a pulse, the
second detuned), a mixer driven into a soft saturation, a four-pole transistor ladder (or one of nine other
filters, see Filters) at twice the sample rate, envelopes for the amplitude and the filter and a third for
modulation, four LFOs and a modulation matrix (see Modulation), and glide. The oscillators run freely and are never reset on a note, and
each wanders slowly in pitch, as analogue oscillators do; the Drift knob sets how far.

Instead of the two analogue oscillators a row can read a wavetable (Wavetable; Analog is the oscillators): the
same tables as the pad synth's -- the PPG's, the sync and formant sweeps, the glass, vocal and sampled ones -- read
band-limited by two oscillators with the row's detune and drift, into the same mixer and ladder. Table Position
chooses the place in the table, and Table Mod how far the row's modulation sequencer moves it from note to note: a
bright step reads further into the table, so the timbre wanders against the notes as on Stürtzer's Iridium
sequences. The composer gives the counter rows after the main sequence a table now and then (Modern in three rows
of five, Doom in one), and in Modern sometimes the main sequence.

Presets. Every synth page -- the voices, the lead, the drone, the tape keys, the strings, the drums and the
atmosphere -- has 1024 factory presets above its knobs, in sixteen groups of sixty-four ("Ladder Bass",
"Glass Arp", "Cathedral Choir", "Slow Swell", "Solar Wind" ...); the arrows step through them. Each name is
two words: the first runs from dark to bright ("Umbral" ... "Radiant"), the second is the group's own, so
"Umbral Keel" is the darkest and "Radiant Piston" the brightest of the ladder basses. On the voices page a
preset goes to the row chosen on the left. The disk keeps the synth's knobs as they stand under a name of
your own (a small text file in the application data, Ephemeris/Presets); they appear under "User". All rows,
on the voices page, sends a preset to every row at once. The effect pages (Echo + Spring, Hall) keep presets
of your own too, each the whole page -- all its rooms -- as it stands. A preset sets the sound, never the mix: level, pan, the sends,
the low cut, the distance and the drone's auto pan stay, and so do the atmosphere's amounts, which the
composer moves. Nor does it put a synth out of tune with the rest: the drone and the bass groups keep their
oscillators within a cent or three of each other (the pure foundation), the others within twelve, the lead's
vibrato and the tapes' wobble stay inside their usual bounds, and no preset transposes anything.

Every piece has its own sounds. With Composer's Sounds on (the Master page; on by default) the composer
chooses a factory preset for every synth in every piece, from the groups that fit the part: the bass row
from Ladder Bass, Deep Ostinato, Dark Throb or Warm Unison, kept dark enough; the main sequence from Squelch
Arp, Resonant Sweep, Pluck Sequence, Tape Sequence, Staccato Pulse or Accent Ratchet, with resonance and a
deep envelope; the other counter rows from the glass, hollow and plucked groups; the lead and the kit by the
style, the tape keys from the style's tape set -- darker in Doom, brighter in Melodic. The preset bar of
each synth page shows "this piece: ..." with the name where the piece now plays, the mixer shows it under
each strip's name, and reroll sounds draws others while every note stays. The composer sets the preset on the
synth's knobs as the piece begins, as a program change: the page shows what plays, and a knob you turn moves from
the preset's value. A synth whose sound a new piece keeps -- after a reroll of the rows or the form, say -- keeps
your knobs as you left them; a new sound replaces them. In a concert each piece brings its sounds as it begins (a
jump into another piece takes that piece's, a jump inside one leaves your knobs). A saved set and a saved session
keep the knobs as they were, the sounds among them. Off, every piece plays your knobs as they are.

The knobs the player's hands move during a piece -- cutoff, resonance, envelope amount, decay -- are
moved by the composer's gestures as offsets from where the piece's settings put them, so the ranges you
choose keep their meaning.

== Filters | | - ==

Every voice -- the rows, the lead, the drone -- and the pad synth choose their filter from ten models of the
classic voltage-controlled filters (Filter on the synth's page, in the group Filter):

    Moog Ladder         the transistor ladder, 24 dB: round, fat, the bass and the main sequence
    Prophet             the OTA cascade of the SSM2040 and CEM3320 (and its reissue SSI2140, the Microwave's CEM3389):
                        present, its resonance saturating before its stages
    Juno                the IR3109 of the Juno and Jupiter: cleaner, the pass band thinner with resonance
    Oberheim SEM        the state-variable filter, 12 dB; Filter Mode morphs low pass -> notch -> high pass
    Xpander             the pole mixing of the Xpander and Matrix-12 on the cascade; Filter Mode chooses the response:
                        LP4, LP2, BP2, BP4, HP2, HP4, notch, phaser
    Diode Ladder        the TB-303's and the EMS's: squelchy, its resonance rubbery
    Korg35              the MS-20's Sallen-Key with its diode-limited feedback: screaming at high resonance
    Polivoks            the Soviet filter whose op-amps are slew-limited on purpose: gritty; Filter Mode to band pass
    EDP Wasp            CMOS inverters as integrators: asymmetric, raw
    Comb                a delay line tuned to the cutoff, its feedback damped; Filter Mode flips its sign

Filter FM lets the first oscillator move the cutoff at audio rate, as on the Xpander: metallic, bell-like edges.

How they are made. Each model is its circuit's differential equation, with the nonlinearities where the circuit
has them -- the transistor pairs of a ladder, the OTAs of a cascade, the diode pairs of the diode ladder, the diode
limiter in the Korg35's feedback, the slew-limited op-amps of the Polivoks, the inverters of the Wasp. The
capacitors are integrated by the trapezoidal rule in the form of the topology-preserving transform (Zavalishin, "The
Art of VA Filter Design"; Simper's equivalent capacitor currents), and the implicit equation this gives is solved
every sample by Newton-Raphson on its exact Jacobian -- the delay-free loop resolved rather than broken by a sample's
delay (D'Angelo and Välimäki 2014 for the ladder; Holters and Zölzer 2015 for nonlinear state-space models of
circuits; Stinchcombe's analyses of the ladder, the diode ladder and the MS-20). The voices run at twice the sample
rate, which keeps the aliasing of the saturating stages low. In the small-signal range each model follows its
circuit's linear response to within 0.15 dB; at full resonance the ladders, the cascades, the diode ladder and the
Korg35 oscillate at their cutoff.

The factory presets carry the filters of their instruments: the basses on the Moog ladder and now and then the
diode ladder, the squelch on the diode ladder and the Korg35, glass on the Xpander's band and high passes, the
Juno strings on the Juno, the Oberheim brass on the SEM, the dark and dusty sounds on the Polivoks and the Wasp, the
cosmic drips on the comb and the phaser -- their cutoffs set so a group keeps its brightness whatever it drew.

== Oscillators | | - ==

Every voice -- the rows, the lead, the drone -- chooses its oscillators (VCO, in the group Oscillators): its own
analog pair, or one of five classic VCOs.

    Analog          the voices' own: ideal ramps and pulses
    Moog 921        the Moog modular's oscillator: a discrete reset whose soft knee rounds the ramp's top; its pitch
                    wanders the most and the slowest
    Prophet-5       the SSM2030 of the Prophet-5: an integrated, nearly ideal core, steady -- and the sync sweeps
    Oberheim SEM    a discrete core whose leakage bends the ramp towards the capacitor's charging curve: round, warm
    ARP 2600        the 4027's fast reset, the ramp speeding up a little towards the top; the most jitter
    E-mu Modular    precise and stable: ideal ramps, half the others' drift, a tenth of their jitter

Every one of them is a sawtooth core -- a capacitor charged by the exponential converter's current until a comparator
fires and a transistor empties it -- and a comparator on the ramp for the pulse. What sets the models apart is what
the circuit does to that: the ramp's bow (the core's leakage), the knee at its top (the reset transistor starting to
conduct early), and above all how the pitch moves -- the drift's spread and speed, how far a note lands off its
pitch, and a fast jitter of a fraction of a cent correlated over a few milliseconds. Honestly: the waveforms differ by
a few tenths of a dB in the upper harmonics and in the phases of the lower ones; what the ear notices first is the
wandering. The magnitudes follow the circuits' principles and the instruments' reputations; they are not measured on
the instruments.

Sync restarts VCO 2 with every cycle of VCO 1 (hard sync, the Prophet's Osc A to Osc B). Osc 2 Pitch sets VCO 2's
interval, -12 to +24 semitones; with sync it moves the timbre instead of the pitch, and the matrix's target Osc 2
Pitch from the Filter Env is the Prophet's Poly-Mod sweep -- the tearing, vocal sync lead. Cross Mod puts VCO 1 on
VCO 2's frequency at audio rate, up to three octaves: the clangorous edges of the ARP 2600's FM and of the Poly-Mod
from Osc B.

How they are made. The waveform's every discontinuity -- the reset, the pulse's edges, a sync restart -- is found
exactly inside its sample step, and the jump and the change of slope there are smoothed with the polynomial BLEP and
its integral, the BLAMP (Välimäki, Pekonen and Nam 2012; Esqueda, Välimäki and Bilbao 2016), at the voices' twice the
sample rate. A synced tone of 1.2 kHz keeps its aliases 53 dB under its harmonics in the audible band (a naive
restart: 19 dB). A voice on a model, with sync or cross mod runs its oscillators beside the SIMD lanes; the Analog
pair stays in them.

The factory presets carry the oscillators of their instruments: the basses on the Moog (the deep ones now and then on
the steady E-mu), the sync sweeps on the Prophet (Bright Stab, Screaming Filter, Cosmic Siren), the round pulses and
the soft pads on the SEM, the plucks and the squelch on the ARP, glass and bells on the E-mu, some with cross mod. The
pad synth's analog pads read the models' waves (see Poly).

== Modulation | | - ==

Every synth has full envelopes, LFOs and a modulation matrix: the rows' voices, the lead, the drone and the pad synth
the whole set, the tape keys and the strings a smaller one.

Envelopes. Amp Envelope: Attack, Decay, Sustain and Release (on the voices the Release is the Amp Release that was
there before). Filter Envelope: Attack, Decay -- the large knob, the one the hands move -- Sustain, Release, and
Release = Decay: on, as by default, the release takes the decay's time, as with the Minimoog's decay switch, and
with no sustain the decay simply runs on through the note's end, so a sequencer's short gates leave the filter's
decay to the hand; off, the Release knob counts. Env Velocity lets the note's velocity scale the filter envelope. On
the pad synth the filter follows the amplitude envelope (Filter Follows Amp) until you switch it off; then it has
four times of its own. Mod Envelope is a third ADSR, for the matrix.

LFOs. Four per voice (two on the tape keys and the strings): Rate; Shape -- sine, triangle, saw up, saw down,
square, sample & hold, smooth random; Sync -- free at Rate, or a division of the bar: 4, 2 or 1 bars, 1/2 to 1/16,
quarter and eighth triplets, and a synced LFO keeps its phase on the piece's beat, so a one-bar sweep opens on every
downbeat; Retrig -- every note starts it from the beginning; Fade -- it comes in over that many seconds after each
note, as a hand reaching for the wheel. Every voice has its own LFOs, and on the pad synth every key has its own
four, so the keys of a chord move apart.

The matrix. Eight slots (four on the tape keys and the strings), each a source, a target and an amount from -1 to 1
(double-click: none). The sources: LFO 1 to 4, Mod Env, Filter Env, Velocity, and Mod Lane -- the step of the row's
modulation sequencer under the note. The targets, and what an amount of 1 means:

    Pitch           12 semitones; the amount counts squared, so the first tenth of the knob holds a vibrato of a few cents
    Pulse Width     0.45 of the duty cycle
    Wave            the whole blend from saw to pulse
    Table Position  the whole wavetable
    Cutoff          five octaves
    Resonance       the whole knob
    Filter Mode     the SEM's morph, the Polivoks' band pass, the comb's sign (the Xpander's response stays the knob's)
    Filter FM       the whole knob
    Level           doubled at +1, shut at -1
    Pan             from one side to the other

Slots on the same target add up. A target a synth does not have does nothing: the pad synth has no pulse width,
blend or filter FM; the tape keys and the strings reach Pitch, Tone, Level and Pan. The tape keys' envelope lies
over the machine's own: Swell (at its minimum, 1 ms, only the pressure pad's rise), Decay, Sustain Level, and
Release -- the fall after the key, the machine's 70 ms by default; the tapes still run out after eight seconds. The
strings keep their Crescendo and Release and have a Decay and a Sustain Level between them.

Everything runs on the piece's clock and the synth's own count of samples, never on the host's blocks: the envelopes
every sample, the LFOs and the matrix at the synth's control steps (every 4 samples on the voices, 16 on the pad
synth, 32 on the tape keys and the strings), and what they move reaches the voices per sample; the random shapes draw
from a stream of their own, so modulation never shifts a voice's drift. A render is the same to the bit for any block
size, and with the matrix empty every synth sounds exactly as it did without it.

The factory presets use it generously: nine in ten carry modulation, on average two slots of the matrix, over ten
different targets on the voices. Each group has its own -- the modulation that makes it what it is -- and every
preset draws one to three more from its synth's pool: the cutoff drifting or pulsing with the bars, sample and hold,
the velocity and the row's modulation lane on filter and timbre, a pluck of the mod envelope, pulse width modulation,
a wobble or a late vibrato, the pan wandering, the SEM's morph and the resonance breathing, a gate of the level in
time, a zap of filter FM; on the pad synth the table walking and every key its own place and pitch. Every one gets
the next free LFO and slot, so they never step on each other; the foundation -- the bass groups and the drone --
never moves its pitch. Among the groups' own: plucks and stabs decay under a held gate; the hollow pulses and the humming drones get their
pulse width modulation; the resonant sweeps a filter sweep synced to two or four bars; the cosmic drips a
sample-and-hold on the cutoff or a pitch blip from the mod envelope; the tape sequences and the dusty solos a wobble;
the legato glides, the bowed pads and the violins a vibrato that comes in late; the PPG and morph pads a slow walk
through their tables; the Oberheim brass a filter swell of its own. The composer's sounds bring them into a piece
with the presets.

== Lead | lead | 4 ==

The lead is the same voice as a row's, with vibrato that comes in over the first half second of a held
note, as a hand reaches the wheel. It plays in its own section and over the peak: phrases of four to
eight bars with rests of two to four between them, on the pentatonic of the moment (with the sixth where
the mode is Dorian), chord tones on the strong beats, glides from the tone below, repeats of its own
phrases, and an end on the fifth or the minor third rather than the root. It plays a little behind the
beat, as a player over a machine does.

== Drone | drone | 5 ==

The drone is the ground under the whole piece: the root, low, held from the start to the fading coda,
following the key when a phase changes it. It is a modular voice as well and has the lead's table.
Auto Pan lets it wander slowly across the stereo field, on a sine of 0.05 Hz (twenty seconds from one
side to the other and back); the lead has the knob as well, at zero. The drone's two oscillators are not
detuned against each other, nor are the bass row's: the foundation of a piece stays in pure octaves and
fifths, without a slow cloud of beating in the middle over the minutes; the detune belongs to the counter
rows, the lead and the pads.

== Tape Keys | tape | 7 ==

A tape keyboard, synthesised: every key has its own strip of tape, with its own small detune, level
and onset lag, played by one capstan whose speed wanders (wow and flutter) and sags a little under
many keys. A tape runs out after eight seconds, as the instrument's did. Three sets: a choir of
several singers through the formants of a sung vowel, strings, and flute. Age puts more of the
machine's character into the sound; the tape set can change between pieces.

Spread widens the mono keyboard without colouring it: the side is the keyboard through a chain of all-passes
whose phase turns at corners spread over the band, added on one side and taken away on the other, so the
two sides differ in phase, not in tone, and their sum -- what a mono speaker plays -- is the keyboard alone.
At the default 0.7 the correlation of the two sides is about 0.35: wide, with a middle the chords stand on.

== Strings | strings | 8 ==

A string machine of the seventies, after the example of Waldorf's Streichfett: top-octave generators
divided down into five registers per key -- sawtooths at 16', 8' and 4', squares at 8' and 4' -- through an
ensemble of bucket-brigade delays swept by a slow and a fast modulation. Keys glide from where they were
when a chord is stolen, as the machine's did.

Registration morphs continuously through eight mixes of the registers: Violins, Violas, Cellos, Basses,
Full, Hollow, Brass and Organ, each with its own brightness and all equally loud. Animate lets a slow sine
move the registration by itself, up to three and a half mixes either way at the Animate Rate, so the blend
of the voices wanders under a held chord; the composer's hands move the registration now and then as well.
Octave Balance tilts the footages towards the low or the high ones. Ensemble Type chooses the chorus: Solina
(three lines, a slow and a fast sweep), Chorus (two lines, one sweep) or Wide (three longer, deeper lines,
spread wider). Phaser sends the machine through four swept all-passes, the classic swirl of strings
through a phase shifter.

== Poly | poly | 6 ==

The pad synth: the polysynth plane of the modern Berlin School -- the Oberheim and Juno pads under Stürtzer's,
Quaeschning's and Redshift's sequences, and the glassy planes of the PPG Wave and the Waldorf wavetable synths.
Eight keys, each with two oscillators a few cents apart and spread across the stereo field, reading one
wavetable; a 12 dB state-variable low pass (the Oberheim SEM's kind) that follows the key by half and the
envelope by Env Amount; a slow envelope (the guide's attack of one to four seconds, release of three to eight);
and an ensemble after it, one chorus line read on either side with its sweep turned against itself -- width that
keeps its middle.

A wavetable is a stack of single cycles; Position chooses where in the stack the oscillators read, and Scan
moves the position with a slow sine at Scan Rate, each key a little apart in its phase -- the one slow modulation
a pad needs. On the PWM table (the default) the position is the pulse width, so the scan is the pulse width
modulation of an analog pad; on a PPG or Waldorf table the timbre walks through its frames. The tables are read
band-limited at eight resolutions, so a high note never aliases. Fifty-one tables: eight from formulas --
Classic (sine, triangle, saw, square, pulse), PWM, Sync (a saw hard-synced to one to five times its master),
Formant (a resonant peak climbing through a saw), Vocal, Organ, Glass and Metal -- and thirty-eight sampled
single cycles from the AmbientSynth's library: its own bowed, tube, vocal, consonant, overtone, morph and
sampled tables, nine of the PPG Wave's tables as the users of WaveEdit Online rebuilt them, and a few of the
Adventure Kid waveforms (AKWF, by Kristoffer Ekstrand; WaveEdit Online and AKWF are CC0); and the waves of the five
classic VCOs (921, Prophet, SEM, 2600 and E-mu Waves; see Oscillators), each model's ramp morphing into its square
and then into a narrowing pulse -- the analog pads read them now and then instead of the PWM table.

The composer brings the pads in where the other planes leave room: in the atmosphere under the drone, from the
first build until the tape keys come in, in the breakdowns and the bridges -- never as a third plane beside the
tape keys and the strings, and an octave under the strings where they play (the production guide's rule). Four
pieces in five of Drift have them, fewer than half of Doom. Its 1024 presets come in sixteen groups -- Analog Pad,
Juno Strings, Oberheim Brass, Sync Sweep, Formant Pad, Vocal Pad, Glass Pad, Single Cycle Pad, Bowed Pad, Tube Pad,
PPG Choir, PPG Upper, Overtone Pad, Morph Pad, Sampled Air, Dark Drone Pad -- and the composer's sounds choose
among them by style.

== Atmosphere | atmos | 9 ==

Wind (filtered noise in a moving band), slow sweeps, and bursts of sample-and-hold bleeps on the notes
of the scale: the space at the start of a piece and in the bridges, and the stars over the builds.

Grains is a granular cloud: short tones, each a sine under a soft window of 40 to 250 ms, on the notes of
the scale over the current root two to three octaves up, each at its own place in the stereo field, as
many per second as Grain Density says. A piece has the cloud with the style's chance (the Style page);
the hands then raise it in the atmosphere, the bridges and the coda, and take it away when the rows come
in.

== Echo + Spring | echo spring delay | 11 ==

A tape echo with a moving read head -- wow, flutter, saturation and loss of highs in the feedback --
synchronised to the tempo, with a ping-pong option; its feedback can run into self-oscillation, which
the echo throw uses. Springs are built into it, as in the tape echoes of the time: they take the echo's
send and return beside it. The composer keeps the bass and the pads out of the echo -- the bass dry, the
tape keys and the strings in the hall only -- and the hands lean the feedback high in the quiet parts of
a piece and low at the peak. Low Cut is a high pass inside the loop, so every repeat is thinner than the
one before, as a distant sound is; Duck lowers the return (of both echoes) while the rows play. The repeats
of both echoes sit at about 70 % of the stereo base rather than hard left and right, so the middle keeps
a share of each and the ping-pong survives in mono.

Echo switches the same send to a bucket-brigade delay instead of the tape: an analogue delay of 4096 stages
as in the pedals of the late seventies. Its clock runs slower the longer the delay is set, and its filters
have to follow the clock, so the longer the echo, the darker its repeats; the clock wanders slowly (Wow
sets how far), and the compander saturates and hisses faintly in the loop. Time, feedback, tone, drive and
ping-pong work as on the tape.

Echo 2 is a second, cleaner tape echo on a send of its own (the rows' Echo 2 Send), with its own time,
feedback, tone, ping-pong and return; its repeats go into the hall as the first echo's do. It lets each
layer of the sequence have its own delay: the main sequence on the first echo's dotted eighths, the other
counter rows on the second in eighths or quarter triplets (1/4T, which both echoes offer), as the composer
draws it for each piece.

== Hall | reverb blend early shimmer | 12 ==

A long, dark hall (an eight-line feedback delay network) with its size, decay, damping, pre-delay and
the band it returns -- by default 250 Hz to 6.5 kHz, so the hall adds space without mud or glare. The
composer lets its decay grow in the atmosphere, the bridges and the coda and shrink by 40 % at the peak,
and sets the pre-delay to a 64th note at the piece's tempo (32 ms at 118 BPM), which keeps the dry sounds in
front of their room. Duck lowers the hall's return by up to that many dB while the rows play, so the tail
blooms in the gaps and steps back under the notes.

The blend room is a second, short plate (Blend Decay about a second, a few ms of pre-delay, 240 Hz to 7 kHz)
on a send of its own (Blend Send on the rows, the lead, the drone, the tape keys, the strings and the drums).
Into Hall sends a share of its return on into the hall, so the far room sounds like the near one going on,
not like a second place -- the serial far space of the dark-ambient practice. The composer puts the second
layer there -- the counter rows, the lead, the drums -- with only a little hall; the pads, the drone and the
atmosphere stay in the hall, the bass stays dry; at the peak the feed into the hall drops to 5 %, where the
far room would fill up. Both returns are ducked by the rows alike.

With the early reflections (send A) and the shimmer (send D) the four rooms of the production guide are complete.
The early reflections are a room without its tail: twelve reflections a side within Early Size (35 ms), 200 Hz
to 8 kHz -- distance and glue for the counter rows, the lead and the drums, with nothing that smears. The
shimmer is the effect hall: a long plate (Shimmer Decay, 8 s) whose output, shifted up an exact octave, is fed
back into it (Shimmer, 0.35), so every round of the tail is an octave brighter and fainter; 400 Hz to 4 kHz.
The composer opens it for the drone, the atmosphere and the tape keys in the atmosphere, the bridges and the
coda, and closes it while the machine runs. Being an octave, it never detunes anything.

Room switches the same send to a plate instead: Dattorro's plate reverberator (1997) -- four diffusers and a
tank of two halves that feed each other, each with a slowly moving all-pass, a damping low pass and the
decay -- denser and brighter than the hall and without a room's early reflections, the sound of the
plates on the records of the seventies. Decay, damping, pre-delay and the band work on it as well; its
return is matched to the hall's level, so the switch changes the room, not the loudness.

== Drums | drums | 10 ==

A small analogue kit for the styles that have drums: kick, snare, hats, toms tuned to the root, rim and
shaker, with patterns per style. The kit comes late in a piece, from the second build to the end of the
peak, and never before 30 % of the piece or after 90 %: the sequence is there before it and stays after
it.

Before the kit, a pulse carries the sequence in most pieces: a soft kick four to the floor in Melodic and
Modern, a heartbeat in Cosmic, a slow beat on one and three in Drift, a low tom once a bar in Doom. It
comes with the second build (not before a fifth of the piece) and makes way for the kit or the breakdown;
Melodic has it in four pieces out of five, Drift in one out of three.

== Master | master | 14 ==

The master: its level, a gentle bus compressor that keeps the music's dynamics (a ratio of at most 1.5),
and a true-peak limiter as protection at -1 dBTP. Before them the mix bus removes DC and subsonics under
20 Hz, keeps everything under 100 Hz in mono whatever its pan, and widens what lies above 300 Hz by Width
(dB of side) -- never so far that the side comes within 3 dB of the mid, so the correlation stays positive
and the mix survives in mono. Mono sums both sides after the limiter, to check the mix as a small speaker
would play it. Soft Clip rounds the peaks above the ceiling into at most that many dB before the limiter
(with anti-aliasing), so short ratchets do not make the limiter work; quieter material passes untouched. Sub
Ceiling holds the band under 80 Hz on its own under that level (a Linkwitz-Riley split), so the sub never
pushes the whole mix into the limiter. The composer moves the level along the form instead of compressing it -- the spaces 3 dB
under the peak, the entry and the breakdown 1.5 dB -- and the width too: widest in the spaces, narrow
where the dry bass comes in, growing again with the layers; while the lead plays, the tape keys and the
strings step back 3 dB.

The styles are levelled to the loudness a streamed release is played at (EBU R128, measured with the
offline renderer on ten-minute pieces): Cosmic, Melodic and Modern about -15 LUFS integrated, Doom and
Drift about -17.5. Since the pieces are drawn anew each time -- how many rows, which presets, whether the drums come
in -- two pieces of one style could lie four dB apart; now every piece's loudest part is measured and brought to
its style's level (at most 4 dB either way): the composer marks the peak, the program renders twenty
seconds of it, measures them, sets the correction on the master, measures again with it (the bus compressor and the
limiter come after the gain) and puts the rest right. That is some seconds of work: the piece begins at once, and
the correction follows while it plays and glides in over a few seconds (the offline renderer measures before it
renders). A concert's pieces each get their own, gliding from one to the next over eight bars. The correction is part
of the piece: an export carries it from the first sample, and your master level comes on top. The loudest three seconds around -12 LUFS, the true peak at -1 dBTP, the peak-to-loudness
ratio 11 to 14 dB and the loudness range 8 to 13 LU -- the dynamics are the product. eph_render prints these
figures for everything it renders, with the stereo correlation and how far the side lies under the mid.
Below, the composer's settings -- the style, key, scale, tempo, lengths and whether the style chooses the
tempo -- which the top bar also shows.

Album Form makes a concert an album: an interlude of three to five minutes between the long pieces -- no
rows, only the drone, a slow chord every 16 to 32 bars on the strings or the tape keys, the atmosphere with
its grains, a longer hall and the open fifth at the end -- the piece where the concert's middle falls the
darkest (Phrygian, darker voices, a row more at the peak), and the last one ethereal (Dorian, a longer hall,
a row fewer, no drums, a quieter lead). Without it a concert is what it was.

Motion keeps the depth awake: a spectrum that does not change for twenty seconds loses its depth to the ear.
The pads' levels move by a few tenths of a dB within a second or two; their hall sends, the strings' tone
and the echo's feedback over 13 to 30 seconds; the atmosphere swells by 6 dB over 41 seconds -- periods that
never lock into the sequence's cycles, so foreground and background do not breathe together. In the
atmosphere, the bridges and the coda, where no sequence plays, a near event sounds now and then: a lead
fragment of one to three notes, 20 to 90 seconds apart. Sub Solo leaves only what lies under 80 Hz, for
checking the bottom of the mix; Mono and Sub Solo are for listening and change nothing that is exported
unless they are on.

Exports (the plugin's and eph_render's) end with twenty seconds of the rooms after the last note, faded in
over two seconds and out over the last ten, never linearly, so the last tail never breaks off. eph_render
--archive writes an archive master besides: no limiter, 24 bit, the whole scaled to a true peak of -3 dBTP
(it renders twice). The report adds the crest factor (true peak over RMS, 17 to 20 dB for the styles; 12 is
the least that keeps the transients) and, with --stems, the correlation of each stem: the rows are mono
points (1.0), the tape keys spread by all-pass decorrelation (about 0.35), the rooms enveloping (about 0.2).

OSC Cues sends what the score knows to a visualiser such as Kaleidoscope, over UDP at the OSC Port, to the
machine named by the environment variable EPH_CUE_HOST (this machine without it): every beat with the
tempo (/eph/beat), every section with its phase (/eph/phase), every new root (/eph/key) and every
conjunction of the rows with the share of the running rows that meet (/eph/conjunction). Each cue leaves
when its sound is heard, not when it is composed.

== Export | cue | 16 ==

What leaves the instrument, in one place, as every generator of the family has it: Export writes what plays as a
24-bit WAV and a MIDI file beside it, Export with stems adds a WAV per channel strip and one for the rooms, the disk
and the folder save and load a piece as a small .ephset file (the seed, the lengths, the rerolls and every changed knob). The
score cues tell a visualiser such as Kaleidoscope over OSC what the score knows, at the moment it is heard.

== Style | custom | 17 ==

The five style profiles side by side, the chosen one lit. A style is not a sound but a set of ranges and
chances the composer draws from: the tempo, the length and the number of sequence phases, how much of the
piece is atmosphere before the rows and after them, how often a later phase changes tempo or key, how many
rows play at the peak and which lengths they are drawn from, how often a row mutates, how slowly the
transposer moves, how often each layer comes in, how dense the lead is, how fast the hands move, how dark
the voices are, how long the hall rings and how loud the piece is. Two pieces of one style share these,
not their notes. The tempi and the levels were set after measuring reference recordings.

The sixth column is a style of your own. "Copy ... into Custom" takes the chosen style's numbers as a
starting point; change what you like on the right, and turn on Use Custom Style: the composer then takes
your numbers. The counter rows' lengths, the transposer, the tape set and the drum patterns still come
from the chosen style -- Cosmic and Drift have no drum patterns, so there Drums Chance does nothing. Your
style is saved with the set and the plugin's state, and a concert starts from it (and morphs from it).

== With a DAW and other apps | | - ==

Four things connect Ephemeris to the software around it and to the rest of the family (02.10.2026). The outputs, Link
and the jam are off until they are wanted, the MIDI goes nowhere until a track takes it, and only the jam -- as a
follower -- changes what the music sounds like.

MIDI out: in a DAW the plugin also sends what it plays -- every note of every part at the moment it sounds, a channel
per part as in the MIDI file of an export -- and leaves the muted parts out. Record it on a MIDI track to keep a part
as notes, or route it to another instrument to double it. A stop or a jump of the transport sends all notes off.

Outputs of their own: besides the main output the plugin has a stereo output for each of the fifteen channel strips -- the eight rows, the lead, the drone, the tape keys, the strings, the poly synth, the drums and the atmosphere -- and one for the rooms, the stems an export writes. They are off to
begin with; switched on in the DAW (in most hosts: the plugin's output configuration, or its multi-output version),
each carries its part before the master, while the main output plays on as before.

Ableton Link (the standalone; Settings > Ableton Link): with another Link app on the network -- Live, Bitwig, a phone
app, another generator of the family -- Ephemeris takes the session's tempo, lines its bars up with the session's and
starts and stops with it; alone in a session it offers its own tempo to whoever joins. In a DAW the
host's transport leads, as before.

The family jam (Settings > Family jam, in the plugin and in the standalone; off to begin with): the five instruments
of the family -- Totality, Parhelion, Ephemeris, Phosphene and Noctuary -- play as one band on the local network. One
leads: its key, the energy of its sections, its breaks and its drops go out to the others. Those set to Follow take
them up -- in Ephemeris: every voice but the drums moves to the leader's key, on top of its own Transpose; the rows'
filters close with a quiet section of the leader's (while the performer's hand leaves them alone); and in the leader's
breaks the drums are out until its drop. A new root comes in at the follower's next bar line, by the shortest way, the
mode with its next piece. Tempo and bars come from Ableton Link in the standalone (switch it on in each of them) or
from the DAW's transport, so that a section the leader announces lands on the same bar everywhere. As the leader
Ephemeris says its piece's key and, a beat ahead, each phase -- the atmosphere, the entry, the build, the peak, the
breakdown, the coda --, with its energy. The jam stays on this network (UDP multicast to 239.255.77.77, port 9177, not
past the router), instruments on one machine find each other as well, and a follower that has not heard its leader for
four seconds goes back to its own key and its own form. The last line of the submenu says what it does: off, leading,
or whom it follows.

On a Mac: every release has a build for Apple Silicon (macOS 12 or newer), the standalone, the VST3 and the Audio Unit (Logic, GarageBand, MainStage; passed by Apple's auval), built and
tested on GitHub's machines. It is signed ad hoc, not notarized: macOS asks once (README-macOS.txt in the zip says
how), and it has not yet been played on a real Mac -- reports are welcome.

On Linux: every release has an archive for x86-64 (glibc 2.35 or newer: Ubuntu 22.04, Debian 12, Fedora 36 and later)
with the standalone, the VST3, the LV2 (Ardour, Carla, Reaper, Qtractor) and the renderer, built and tested on GitHub's Ubuntu machines and tried under WSL.
README-Linux.txt in it says where everything goes: the VST3 into ~/.vst3, the LV2 into ~/.lv2, the standalone started where it lies, sound
through ALSA or JACK (PipeWire serves both).

Demos: the release "demos" holds a track per style as an MP3 and one of them as a video, with pictures by
KaleidoscopeEnhanced cut to the track's own score cues; Tools/demo/make_demos.py makes them all again (eph_render).

KaleidoscopeEnhanced understands the score cues of all five instruments as they come (since 02.10.2026) -- Totality's
blocks and keys, Parhelion's sections, Ephemeris' phases, Phosphene's sections and drops, Noctuary's bars, keys and
scenes -- and cuts its pictures to them, on a drop at once.

== Meta Quest | | - ==

The whole generator runs on the headset (bin\quest\EphemerisQuest.apk, see Quest/README.md), played with the hands
in the grammar every generator's headset shares (The panel): left pinch play and stop, right pinch the moves held and
let go, both hands together the next piece; the left hand's height the rows' filters, the right's the echo throw. A
head-locked panel shows the piece, its section, the root and the scale, the time and the two hand controls, the orrery
of the Rack page beside it. A file eph.cfg in the app's folder sets the seed, the style, a piece's length, the quality
and any knob; bridge_host (and bridge_port, 9102) send the hands to Ephemeris on a computer instead, which then plays
with them as with its own; audio=0 leaves the headset silent.
